HNSW: finding the nearest vectors without looking at most of them

Code: day 66 on GitHub

100 Days of Python - day 66

Three thousand vectors, a hundred and twenty queries, ten results each. A brute-force search computes three thousand distances per query and is right by definition. The index I built for this day computes 908 and gets 94.7% of the same answers. That is the entire pitch for approximate nearest neighbour search: you give up a measurable, tunable amount of correctness and you stop looking at 70% of your data.

Day 65 ended at the uncomfortable part - a full scan is a single matrix multiply, which is fine at ten thousand documents and strictly linear after that. This is what replaces the scan.

Read that figure and the algorithm is finished. Every point is a vertex; every vertex is linked to a handful of nearby vertices; the search is a walk. Start somewhere, look at your current point's neighbours, step to whichever one is closer to the query, repeat until no neighbour is an improvement. Those are real edges from a real index and real hops - the amber points are the candidate list the walk kept alive at the bottom, the cyan ones are the answers it returned, and the whole thing computed 36 distances out of 140 points. The teal points it never visited are the only thing an index is for.

Three things make that walk work, and all three are measurable.

The layers

The three panels in the figure are three graphs over the same points. Each point draws a level exactly once when it is inserted, from a geometric distribution: the probability of climbing one more level is 1/M. So layer 0 holds every point, layer 1 holds about a twelfth of them, layer 2 a twelfth of that:

layer   nodes   share    predicted
    0   3,000   1.000      3,000.0
    1     260   0.087        250.0
    2      21   0.007         20.8
    3       5   0.002          1.7

If that sounds familiar, it is a skip list - Day 31 - with a graph instead of a linked list. The express lanes let the walk cross the space in a few hops before it starts looking at detail. The search enters at the single point on the top layer, keeps one candidate alive up there, and drops a layer whenever no neighbour improves.

ef, which is the only knob you can turn in production

At the bottom layer the walk stops being greedy-with-one-candidate and keeps ef of them alive at once, expanding all of their neighbours before deciding. That number is the whole recall story:

ef    distances   fraction of corpus   recall@10
10          319               10.6%       0.629
25          567               18.9%       0.849
50          908               30.2%       0.947
100       1,432               47.7%       0.996
200       2,071               69.0%       1.000

Steep at the start, flat at the end. The important property is not the shape, it is that ef can be changed per query without rebuilding anything. Everything else about an HNSW index is frozen at build time; this one number is how a running service trades latency for recall.

The part I did not expect: the hierarchy does nothing

The H in HNSW stands for hierarchical. So I deleted the hierarchy - kept the bottom graph only, searched it from the same entry point, and then from a randomly chosen point:

with the express lanes                   908 distances   recall 0.947
bottom layer only, same entry point      886 distances   recall 0.947
bottom layer only, random entry point    886 distances   recall 0.947

Nothing got worse. Slightly less work, identical recall, even when the walk starts somewhere arbitrary.

This is not a bug in my implementation; it reproduces a published result (Munyampirwa, Lakshman and Coleman, ECIR 2026), which found no measurable benefit from the hierarchy on high-dimensional data and a 38-39% saving in peak construction memory at a hundred million vectors when it is removed. Their explanation is the hub highway: in high dimensions a small number of points end up in almost everybody's neighbour list, so the bottom graph already contains the long edges the layers were supposed to provide. Earlier work found the layers only help below roughly 32 dimensions - which is to say, below the dimensionality anyone actually uses.

I am keeping the layers in the code because that is what hnswlib, FAISS, Qdrant and pgvector all ship, and because the result is recent enough that the libraries have not moved. But it is a good reminder that the famous part of a famous algorithm is not necessarily the part doing the work.

M, which is the price

M is how many neighbours each point keeps - 2M on the bottom layer, where the links matter most.

M    edges/node   link bytes   vs the vector   recall@10
 6         13.1           52           0.27x       0.936
12         24.9          100           0.52x       0.996
20         40.9          164           0.85x       0.999

Recall saturates immediately. The memory does not: at M = 20 the graph costs about as much as the vectors it indexes, and the build - which runs an ef_construction-sized search for every single point inserted - gets steadily slower as M grows. So the question is never "what is the best M", it is "what is the smallest M that reaches the recall I need" - and the answer depends on data you have to measure, not on the default in the README.

There is a second decision hiding inside the insert, and it is the one that surprised me most when I turned it off. When a new point has collected its candidates, which M of them does it keep? The obvious answer - the nearest ones - is wrong. On a corpus of twelve clusters it gives recall 0.754, because a point's nearest neighbours are all in its own cluster, so the graph quietly loses every edge that leaves the cluster and the greedy walk gets locked inside. The actual rule keeps a candidate only if it is closer to the new point than to anything already chosen, which preserves the long edges: recall 0.799, paid for with a slower insert, since every candidate now has to be compared against the ones already kept.

Deleting things, which is not deleting

Removing a point from a graph index properly means repairing every neighbour list that points at it. Nobody does that online. So a delete is a tombstone: the point stays in the graph as a stepping stone and is filtered out of the results.

state                      recall@10   distances/query   spent on deleted points
10% deleted                    0.985               757                     10.2%
30% deleted                    0.974               757                     29.8%
rebuilt after 30%              0.993               666                         -

Look at the middle column. The work per query does not move while the useful corpus shrinks - almost a third of every query's distance computations go to points that cannot be returned. Rebuilding sweeps it away. Every vector database that offers deletion is really offering a compaction schedule, and the only question is who runs it and when.

Six times the data, twice the work

vectors   distances/query   fraction of corpus   recall@10
    500               446                89.1%       0.998
  1,000               644                64.4%       0.993
  2,000               824                41.2%       0.972
  3,000               908                30.2%       0.947

Brute force is strictly proportional to the corpus; the walk grows like its logarithm, which is the entire reason an index exists. Notice the recall drifting down in the same table: a bigger graph means a longer way to walk, so ef has to be re-tuned when a corpus grows, not only when it changes. The index that was measured at launch is not the index running six months later.

Four ways this fails without telling you

ef smaller than k. ef is how many candidates stay alive, so it is also a ceiling on how many answers can exist. With ef = 5 and k = 10 the walk cannot return ten results at all; libraries clamp ef up to k, which hides the error and still only reaches 0.629.

Inner product on unnormalised vectors. Scale a quarter of the corpus by three and recall against the true cosine answer falls to 0.250, with every hit coming from the scaled group. The index is not broken, it is answering a different question.

Grading the index against itself. Recall measured against the index's own output is 1.000; against brute force it is 0.947. An approximate index always needs a non-approximate reference, which means keeping a small exact ground-truth set around.

Never compacting. See above.

None of the four raises an exception. All four return a plausible list of plausible documents. The only way to notice is to measure recall against something exact.

The three numbers, under four spellings

import hnswlib
ix = hnswlib.Index(space="cosine", dim=d)
ix.init_index(max_elements=N, M=16, ef_construction=200)
ix.set_ef(64)
ix.mark_deleted(i)

index = faiss.IndexHNSWFlat(d, 32)   # 32 is M
index.hnsw.efConstruction = 200
index.hnsw.efSearch = 64

Chroma calls them hnsw:M, hnsw:construction_ef, hnsw:search_ef. Qdrant calls them m, ef_construct, hnsw_ef. Milvus calls them M, efConstruction, ef. pgvector puts the first two in CREATE INDEX and the third in SET hnsw.ef_search. Pinecone exposes none of them, because the index type is the product. In every case it is the same three decisions this article measured: how much memory the graph costs, how carefully the neighbour lists were built, and how much recall you want per query.

What the managed services actually take away is not the algorithm. It is the compaction schedule.
